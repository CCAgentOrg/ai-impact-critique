Methodology

Red Team Analysis Process

This counter-narrative was developed through a systematic red-team assessment of the AI Impact Commons website (aiimpactcommons.global) conducted in February 2026. Our process consisted of the following phases:

Phase 1: Inventory and Categorization

  • Fetched the main site and the impact stories directory.
  • Extracted all listed deployments with their titles, countries, years, and short descriptions.
  • Grouped deployments into thematic categories (Agriculture, Education, Healthcare, Environment, Other).

Phase 2: Identification of Concerning Use Cases

  • Applied a harm framework focusing on: surveillance, data extraction, algorithmic bias, exclusion, function creep, and potential for abuse.
  • Selected the top 10 use cases that exhibited the highest concentration of red flags, particularly those involving vulnerable populations (farmers, women, patients, students, job seekers) and sensitive data (biometrics, health, voice, location).

Phase 3: Research and Source Gathering

  • For each use case, conducted targeted searches to find:
    • Academic papers critiquing similar AI systems (digital colonialism, algorithmic bias, surveillance studies).
    • NGO and civil society reports documenting harms in related deployments.
    • Community voices: quotes from affected individuals, activists, and grassroots organizations in the relevant geographies.
    • Information about resistance networks fighting these harms in each region.
  • Used web search via DuckDuckGo (through web_fetch) to discover credible sources.
  • Fetched full content from key sources (where accessible) to ensure accurate representation.
  • Prioritized sources from the Global South and voices from affected communities.

Phase 4: Analysis and Write-Up

  • For each use case:
    • Replicated the description from AI Impact Commons verbatim (as listed).
    • Expanded on potential harms, connecting to broader patterns observed across deployments.
    • Provided properly formatted citations with URLs, authors, and dates where available.
    • Curated community quotes that illustrate the human rights stakes.
    • Listed organizations actively resisting these technologies, with brief descriptions of their work.
    • Formulated key demands for remedy.

Critical Frameworks Applied

Our analysis drew on several established frameworks:

  • Digital Colonialism: The extraction of data and digital labor from the Global South for the benefit of the Global North, mirroring colonial patterns (Binary et al., 2025; Ndaka et al., 2025).
  • Data Justice: The principle that data practices should protect marginalized groups, ensure fairness, and promote community control (Couldry & Meese, 2020).
  • Algorithmic Accountability: The need for structural responses to algorithmic power, not just technical audits (AI Now Institute, 2023).
  • Indigenous Data Sovereignty: The right of communities to govern the collection, use, and sharing of their own data (Te Mana Raraunga, 2020; Global Indigenous Data Alliance).
  • Feminist AI Ethics: Centering the experiences of women and marginalized genders in AI governance, rejecting technological solutionism for complex social issues.

Systemic Red Flags Observed

Across the AI Impact Commons catalog, we noted consistent patterns:

  • Unverified self-reported data: The Commons explicitly does not validate claims or metrics.
  • Vulnerable populations as data sources: Farmers, women, disabled, rural poor, patients are repeatedly targeted for data extraction.
  • Government partnerships as legitimacy: State endorsement enables scaling without public debate.
  • Private profit from public data: Many implementations are private sector companies.
  • Identity system proliferation: Farmer IDs, citizen health IDs, learner profiles create surveillance infrastructure.
  • Export of risks to Global South: Targets LMICs while HQs are in Global North/Singapore.
  • No harm mitigation discussed: Focus on benefits, not risks or opt-out mechanisms.
  • "Replicability" as virtue: Encourages spreading potentially harmful systems without local safeguards.

Limitations and Future Work

Due to resource constraints, we focused on 10 use cases; many others in the Commons also warrant scrutiny. Our source gathering was limited by access to paywalled articles and proprietary white papers. We prioritized open-access sources to keep this project accessible. Future iterations could incorporate more community-led research and expand to other regions.
