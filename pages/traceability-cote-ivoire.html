1. AI-Enabled Traceability & Digital Climate Governance

Location: Côte d'Ivoire
Deployment: TRST01 Global Ventures (Singapore)
Scale: 150,000+ farmers mapped, 84,000 hectares, 60,000 farmers (Côte d'Ivoire alone)
AI Impact Commons Category: Agriculture

Description from AI Impact Commons

"AI-Enabled Traceability and Digital Climate Governance for Deforestation-Free Agriculture and Carbon Markets" addresses the challenge of fragmented smallholder data, deforestation risk, and weak monitoring in global agricultural supply chains. The solution aims to provide traceability for EUDR (European Union Deforestation Regulation) compliance and enable participation in carbon markets.

Expanded Harms & Analysis

While presented as a sustainability solution, this deployment creates multiple risk vectors:

1. Digital Farmer Identity Systems Risk Land Rights Displacement

Mapping 150,000+ farmers and creating digital identities can undermine customary land tenure. In Côte d'Ivoire, only 1% of rural land had formal records before digital registry initiatives (World Bank, 2024). When digital systems prioritize formalized titles, women and marginalized groups who hold land under customary arrangements risk displacement. The World Bank's own PRESFOR program acknowledges that women's land rights are often compromised in formalization processes, requiring specialized "land advocates" to protect them (World Bank, 2024).

2. Carbon Credit Registry Control Enables Green Grabbing

By integrating farmers into carbon market platforms, the same entities that control traceability data can also control carbon credit generation and distribution. This creates a pathway for "green grabbing" — where carbon revenue flows to corporations and intermediaries while smallholders receive minimal benefits. The lack of transparent benefit-sharing mechanisms means farmers who provide the data and adopt sustainable practices may not see commensurate rewards.

3. EUDR Compliance Pressure Forces Corporate-Controlled Traceability

The EU's Deforestation Regulation mandates strict traceability for commodities like cocoa. While environmentally justified, this regulation creates a compliance burden that favors large agribusiness with resources for digital systems. Smallholders must either join corporate-controlled platforms like TRST01 or risk exclusion from EU markets. This consolidates power in the hands of traceability providers and reduces farmer autonomy.

4. Data Extraction Feeds Global AI Systems Without Local Benefit

The farm-level data collected (yields, practices, locations, identities) becomes training data for predictive models, risk assessments, and commodity forecasting. This data flows to servers controlled by foreign companies, contributing to the digital colonialism dynamic where African data powers AI products that primarily serve Global North markets and interests.

5. Surveillance Infrastructure Doubles as Control Mechanism

High-resolution mapping and continuous monitoring create a surveillance capability over agricultural production. This data could be repurposed by governments or corporations to enforce compliance, restrict cropping choices, or seize land under various pretexts. The absence of robust data protection laws in many African countries exacerbates this risk.

Systemic Pattern

This use case exemplifies the "identity system proliferation" red flag noted in our analysis: creating farmer IDs enables state and corporate control of agriculture. It also illustrates the "export of risks to Global South" pattern: the EU benefits from deforestation-free commodities while Côte d'Ivoire bears the costs of surveillance and data extraction.

Networks of Resistance

Organizations fighting for farmer data sovereignty and against digital colonialism in West Africa and globally:

La Via Campesina

Global peasant movement advocating for food sovereignty and farmers' rights. Opposes corporate control of agriculture and data.

GRAIN

International NGO supporting small-scale farmers; research on agribusiness data extraction ("The Great Data Grab").

ETC Group

Monitors corporate control of food, agriculture, and emerging technologies; advocates for farmer data ownership.

Access Now

Global digital rights organization; works on data protection and against digital colonialism in Africa.

Association des Utilisateurs de l'Internet (AUI) - Togo & West Africa

Regional digital rights network promoting internet freedom and data sovereignty.

Coalition for Digital Rights in West Africa

Coalition of civil society organizations advocating for rights-respecting digital policies.

Key Demands

  • Data sovereignty: Farmers must own and control data about their land and production.
  • Free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC): No data collection without community consent.
  • Benefit-sharing: Fair compensation for data that generates commercial value.
  • Right to opt out: Farmers must be able to refuse digitization without losing market access.
  • Transparency: Clear documentation of where data flows and how it is used.
