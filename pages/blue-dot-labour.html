10. Blue Dot Experiment: Labour Market Discovery (India)

Location: India
Deployment: Blue Dot Experiment (likely a platform or research project)
Scale: Job seeker profiles collected at scale; platform gatekeeping potential
AI Impact Commons Category: Other

Description from AI Impact Commons

"Blue Dot Experiment: Labour Market Discovery" uses machine learning to match job seekers to employers. The system aims to improve labor market efficiency by making opportunities more discoverable and reducing frictional unemployment.

Expanded Harms & Analysis

1. Labour Surveillance and Data Extraction

Job seeker profiles include skills, demographics, location, employment history, and sometimes behavioral data (test scores, video interviews). Collecting this at scale creates a massive surveillance apparatus over the workforce. Employers may share data with the platform, which then becomes a central repository of personal and professional information for millions, often without robust consent or oversight.

2. Platform Gatekeeping Controls Employment Access

If Blue Dot becomes the dominant matching platform, it gains gatekeeping power over who gets seen by employers. Its algorithms decide which candidates to recommend, effectively controlling access to job opportunities. Opacity in ranking means discrimination based on protected attributes (caste, gender, age, disability) can be baked into "neutral" scores.

3. Bias Excludes Informal Sector Workers

AI matching systems typically rely on structured data: formal education credentials, past job titles, standardized test scores. India's vast informal sector—where 90% of workers are employed—lacks such documentation. These workers are systematically excluded from algorithmic matching, reinforcing the divide between formal and informal labor markets.

4. Employer Data Sharing Could Enable Wage Collusion and Discrimination

Platforms that aggregate job seeker data also collect data on employer pay scales, requirements, and hiring patterns. In the absence of strong antitrust enforcement, such data could be used to coordinate wages among employers (price-fixing) or to filter out candidates based on group characteristics, all under the guise of "efficiency."

5. Black Box Algorithms Without Accountability

Job seekers have little recourse if they are rejected by an algorithm. There is often no transparency about why a candidate was not shortlisted, no ability to correct errors, and no human review. This violates principles of procedural justice and can entrench historical discrimination patterns.

Systemic Pattern

This use case embodies "vulnerable populations as data sources" (job seekers, many from marginalized backgrounds) and "government partnerships as legitimacy" if the platform is tied to public employment programs. It also illustrates "export of risks to Global South": experimental AI matching could be rolled out rapidly in India with fewer protections than in Europe or the US.

Key Demands

  • Algorithmic transparency: Mandatory disclosure of matching criteria and ranking factors to job seekers.
  • Right to human review: Candidates can request a human recruiter to review their application and provide reasons for rejection.
  • Prohibit data sharing across employers: Prevent platforms from using data from one employer's search to influence matches for others in ways that enable collusion.
  • Include informal work experience: Platforms must accept and value non-formal employment histories, skills from self-employment, and community roles.
  • Worker data cooperatives: Enable job seekers to collectively own and control their employment data, deciding when and how it is shared.
